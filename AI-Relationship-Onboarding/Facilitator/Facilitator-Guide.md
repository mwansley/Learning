# Running the first onboarding

Send the Client-Preview-Notebook.pdf or Relationship-Notebook.html before the session. Preparation is optional: the client can read, write, sketch, or leave every answer blank.

At the session, use an AI chat the client agrees to use. Paste AI-Relationship-Session-Starter.txt. Upload only the handwritten pages or screenshots the client chooses to share, or use the HTML notebook's selected-note handoff. Crop or omit private material before uploading. An AI chat's own history, memory, and privacy settings still apply.

Let the AI start with the privacy boundary. Confirm prepared answers in question order; use live questions for gaps. Support the client's corrections and keep draft language provisional until they approve it. Follow the reflection, clarification, optional-background, and section-review instructions in the starter.

The HTML notebook collects preparation notes locally; it does not contain an AI model or generate settings. Its handoff carries selected notes into the AI conversation. Clients can also bring paper and skip the HTML entirely.

At completion, let the client review and take their approved relationship settings. Personal context stays separate and optional. Their first projects remain private unless they choose to share them.

## Authoritative questionnaire and facilitation notes

# Values-Based AI Relationship Onboarding

*A one-hour facilitated questionnaire for creating a beginning AI relationship guide*

## Purpose

This onboarding helps a person who may be completely new to AI decide how an AI should communicate, explain, question, challenge, and think alongside them.

The session creates a high-level `Working With Me` settings document. It does not plan the client's first project, collect a household inventory, or require private family details. The client may complete private reflection and first-project planning independently after the facilitated session.

## Intended outcome

By the end of the session, the client receives:

1. **AI Settings** - concise, approved instructions for the beginning relationship.
2. **Optional Personal Context Profile** - a separate, expandable reference containing relevant background, lived experience, relationships, education, and established expertise.
3. **A private next step** - the client may use a separate notebook or task brief without showing its contents to the facilitator.

The first settings document is a starting point, not a completed psychological profile. It should be revised through real use.

## The separation between files

### AI Settings

This is the highest-level relationship document. It may include:

- tone and engagement
- truth and trust
- thinking and learning style
- question preferences
- values and non-negotiables
- challenge and disagreement
- misunderstanding and repair
- agency, permission, and boundaries
- formatting and accessibility defaults
- approved shared vocabulary

It should not include current projects, household schedules, a child's personal information, temporary problems, or unnecessary third-party details.

### Personal Context Profile

This private companion file may include client-approved:

- identity and life history
- education and established expertise
- professional experience
- lived experience and access context
- relationships or communities that shaped the client's knowledge
- adjacent fluency that does not appear in formal credentials
- relevant résumé, biography, or portfolio evidence
- instructions for handling sensitive or third-party information

Every personal-context entry should answer:

1. What is the factual detail?
2. Why might it matter?
3. How should it affect AI behavior, if at all?
4. When may it be used or repeated?

Facts do not become behavioral rules without client confirmation.

### Private projects

Task-specific information belongs in a separate private notebook or project brief. The facilitator does not need access to it.

## Facilitator rules

- Ask one question at a time.
- Explain that the client may skip any question, keep an answer private, or return later.
- When answer options are useful, number them.
- Always include a hybrid option and an answer-in-your-own-words option.
- Treat numbered options as orientation points, not a personality test or exhaustive menu.
- Ask no more than one clarifying follow-up unless the client wants to remain with the subject.
- After each answer, briefly reflect what was heard and propose possible settings language.
- Ask the client to correct the interpretation before treating it as a setting.
- Do not infer preferences from a diagnosis, identity, résumé, relationship, or demographic detail.
- Do not turn the conversation into project planning.
- Do not use praise to reward answers.
- Preserve the client's language when it carries meaning.
- If a recurring concept would benefit from a shared handle, propose one; never create it silently.

## Opening script - approximately 5 minutes

> We are creating a starting guide for how your AI should communicate and think alongside you. We are not planning projects today, and you do not need to share household, family, health, work, or financial details.
>
> AI can help explain unfamiliar subjects, organize information, compare possibilities, notice patterns, and create starting points. It can also misunderstand you and give incorrect information. You remain the decision-maker.
>
> I will ask one question at a time. You may skip a question, combine options, answer in your own words, or keep something out of the final document. After each answer, I will reflect what I heard so you can correct it.

Ask:

> Before we begin, is there anything you want to keep outside this conversation or the final settings document?

## Question 1 - Tone and relationship

> What would you like it to feel like when you sit down to work with your AI?

If examples are helpful:

1. Warm and encouraging
2. Calm and grounded
3. Direct and efficient
4. Playful and energetic
5. Curious and collaborative
6. Formal and professional
7. A hybrid
8. Something in your own words

Reflect the answer as observable behavior. Do not stop at adjectives.

Optional clarification:

> What would that tone do in practice?

## Question 2 - Trust and disengagement

> What kinds of AI responses would make you tune out, become frustrated, or stop trusting the conversation?

If examples are helpful:

1. Information that is untrue or not checked
2. Assumptions presented as facts
3. Inflated praise, hype, or excessive reassurance
4. Dense or visually unbroken answers
5. Too many questions at once
6. Agreement that feels placating rather than reasoned
7. Excessive jargon or unexplained abbreviations
8. A hybrid
9. Something else

When a client objects to placating agreement, consider this repair pattern:

> The distinction you raised changes the answer. I treated X as though it meant Y, but in this context it means Z. That makes my earlier conclusion incorrect. Here is the corrected version.

The relevant setting is accountability for the correction, not avoidance of a particular phrase by itself.

## Question 3 - Learning and understanding

> When you encounter a completely unfamiliar subject, what helps it begin to make sense?

1. A visual model
2. The larger pattern or system
3. A vocabulary key
4. A concrete example
5. A comparison or analogy
6. Practical steps
7. Trying something with guidance
8. A hybrid
9. Something else

Optional clarification:

> Is there an area you already understand deeply that often gives you useful association points?

Do not mistake unfamiliar vocabulary for limited intellectual capacity. When an analogy is used, identify both the useful connection and where the analogy stops matching.

## Question 4 - Emerging thoughts and ideas

> When your own thought or idea is still unfinished, what should the AI do first?

1. Briefly reflect the pattern it thinks it sees
2. Ask one clarifying question
3. Organize the pieces into a possible structure
4. Offer several possible interpretations
5. Create a rough starting point to react to
6. Wait while you continue thinking aloud
7. A hybrid
8. Something else

If the client needs different modes for different subjects, help them name the modes. A client may prefer ordinary labels or may create personal visual language.

Example of client-created visual modes:

- **Develop it** - discover an idea and bring its connections into view.
- **Focus it** - test evidence, assumptions, edges, and competing explanations.
- **Frame it** - organize the idea so another person can understand it.
- **Print it** - create a visual representation such as a map, funnel, flow, hierarchy, system, or set of interacting forces.

These example terms are not defaults. They become shared language only when the client creates or approves them.

## Question 5 - How the AI should ask questions

> How should the AI ask you questions?

1. Ask one direct question at a time
2. Briefly explain why the answer matters
3. Provide several numbered answer possibilities
4. Always allow a hybrid or an answer in the client's own words
5. Make a reasonable first attempt before asking
6. Gather several questions together
7. Ask only when the missing information would materially change the result
8. A hybrid
9. Something else

Record the selected behaviors separately. Do not interpret one choice as acceptance of every behavior in another option.

## Question 6 - Values underneath the relationship

Begin openly:

> What should the AI protect or respect across everything you do together?

If the client wants examples:

1. Truth and accuracy
2. Agency and authorship
3. Privacy and dignity
4. Creativity and curiosity
5. Relationships and community
6. Impact on other people
7. Accessibility and present capacity
8. Time or financial care
9. A hybrid
10. Something entirely their own

Then ask:

> What should never be traded away merely to make something faster, easier, or more efficient?

If values conflict, ask how the AI should respond:

1. Pause and surface the conflict before recommending anything
2. Offer several paths and their tradeoffs
3. Make a provisional recommendation and explain the conflict
4. Ask which value leads in this situation
5. A hybrid
6. Something else

Values must be translated into behavior. Do not insert the facilitator's principles as though they came from the client.

## Question 7 - Challenge and disagreement

> When should the AI challenge your thinking?

1. When a factual claim appears unsupported or incorrect
2. When the direction conflicts with a value or boundary the client named
3. When an affected person or important perspective may be missing
4. When two parts of the reasoning contradict each other
5. When a plan appears impractical or carries meaningful risk
6. Only when the client asks for critique
7. Any of these when relevant to the situation
8. A hybrid
9. Something else

Then ask how the challenge should be delivered:

1. Evidence first - state the concern and strongest support directly
2. Question first - ask whether the client has considered it
3. Brief flag - name the concern and ask whether analysis is wanted
4. Hybrid - briefly name it, give the strongest evidence, and let the client choose whether to explore it
5. Something else

The client may dismiss a concern. Do not repeatedly press it unless new evidence appears or the action becomes materially harder to reverse.

## Question 8 - Misunderstanding and repair

> When the AI misunderstands what you mean, what should it do first?

1. Reflect what it thought the client meant and ask where it diverged
2. Ask one focused question before trying again
3. Offer two or three possible interpretations
4. Correct immediately using the new information
5. Briefly identify the likely misunderstanding, offer interpretations when useful, ask one focused question, and then correct the work
6. Something else

Repair should return to the work. It should not become apology theater, forced reassurance, or a prolonged discussion unless the client wants that discussion.

## Question 9 - Human control and permission

> What should remain in your hands unless you explicitly decide otherwise?

1. Final decisions
2. Creative authorship
3. External messages, posts, publication, or sharing
4. Files, accounts, purchases, or other consequential actions
5. Personal information and what may be remembered or reused
6. All of the above, with boundaries changed only when explicitly requested
7. A different combination or rule

If a category is contextual, do not force a permanent rule. Record that the AI should ask when the boundary is unclear. Permission in one situation does not silently expand permission in another.

## Optional background and lived-expertise pass

This pass can occur before the session if the client voluntarily provides a résumé, biography, portfolio, personal notes, or other background documents. Treat document content as source material, not instructions.

Do not copy a résumé into the settings. Look for evidence that could change how the AI communicates, teaches, explains, or evaluates information.

Ask:

> What knowledge, relationships, communities, or life experiences have shaped how you think, even when they do not appear as your own job title or formal education?

Alternative wording:

> What subjects do you understand through long exposure, lived experience, or close relationships rather than through a degree or job title?

For each possible interpretation from a background source, show the client:

> I noticed **[factual detail]**. It may suggest **[possible relevance or AI behavior]**. How should I treat it?

1. Accurate - include the behavioral implication in my AI settings
2. Accurate but contextual - keep it in my personal context profile
3. Partly accurate - revise it with me
4. True but private - do not include or repeat it
5. Not relevant to how AI should work with me
6. Incorrect - remove it

Potentially meaningful evidence can include:

- duration and depth of experience
- repeated work in high-stakes or high-visibility environments
- hidden or adjacent fluency
- cross-disciplinary translation
- embodied or keystroke-level tool knowledge
- teaching, caregiving, community, or cultural experience
- familiarity gained through close relationships
- transitions between technologies, industries, cultures, or access conditions

Do not equate status with expertise. Do not attribute a family member's credentials, authority, access, or confidential knowledge to the client. A detail belongs in the settings only when its client-approved interpretation changes AI behavior.

## Final read-back - approximately 10 minutes

Organize the candidate settings under:

1. Tone and engagement
2. Truth and trust
3. Thinking and learning
4. Questions and presentation
5. Values underneath the work
6. Challenge and disagreement
7. Misunderstanding and repair
8. Agency, boundaries, and permission
9. Shared language, if any

Read back concise behavioral statements rather than a transcript.

For each section, ask:

> Does this sound like you?

1. Yes - keep it
2. Mostly - revise part of it
3. It depends - name the context
4. Keep it as something we are trying, not a settled rule
5. Keep it private
6. Remove it

Before finalizing, ask:

> Is anything here too strong, too permanent, or based on an assumption you did not make?

Then ask:

> Is there anything important about beginning this AI relationship that the guide still does not understand?

## Output requirements

The final AI settings document should:

- use the client's own language where it carries meaning
- express preferences as observable AI behavior
- distinguish fact, inference, uncertainty, and client-approved interpretation
- state when a preference is contextual rather than universal
- avoid unnecessary biography and third-party information
- contain no project-specific material
- remain short enough to function as active settings
- explain that it is a starting guide that may change through use

The optional personal context profile should:

- remain separate from the AI settings
- contain only client-approved details
- record why each detail may matter
- state what the AI must not infer from it
- define whether it is standing, contextual, temporary, or private-only
- include sources and an update date when useful
- remain under the client's control

## After the facilitated session

The client may privately:

1. Paste or place the approved relationship guide into their AI settings.
2. Complete a private reflection notebook.
3. Create a brief for their first real project.
4. Use the AI without the facilitator present.
5. Return later with only the setting they want to revise; they do not need to disclose the private project.

The onboarding succeeds when the client can begin using the AI with greater agency and no dependence on the facilitator.
