## Wanz Approved

A thrift-shopping series built around your “Thrift Shop” vocal: people show you their finds, you notice the details, and you give the verdict.

People show you a secondhand find. You ask about it, point out what you like, and decide whether it earns your approval. The familiar vocal supplies the biggest verdict, used sparingly so people wait for it.

**The format:** Short reviews with three verdicts: a shrug, “That’s a come-up,” and the rare full-voice line. Aim for the top verdict roughly once in five reviews. Record a clean second take.

**Who it’s for:** Start with creators 50+ in thrift, vintage, menswear, and estate-sale communities. Conversations between peers can invite a wider audience to join in.

**How it could earn:** First build a recognizable series. Later possibilities: a paid store appearance, a sponsored episode, or licensing the format and approved brand assets. Each would have its own fee and terms.

**What we would make:** A repeatable seated setup, a contributor’s enabled or agreed clip, one useful observation, three verdict takes, a clean alternative for the highest tier, and a consistent closing question.

- Duet or stitch a haul — respond to someone’s find, starting with creators 50+ in thrift, vintage, and menswear.
- The store visit — a monthly hunt for one good find, with room for a paid store appearance later.
- Viewer submissions — people bring their finds to you for a verdict. Record a reusable original sound so people can join in.

### Sample script

**00–03 · Hook**

Show the contributor’s item or your own jacket. Cut immediately to your expression.

“Hold on. Show me the lining.”

**03–10 · The detail**

A close view of the actual feature. Describe what catches your eye.

“The outside got my attention. That detail is what made me stop.”

**10–15 · The story**

Let the owner explain the price or reason they kept it. If a solo episode, give the true answer yourself.

“What made you take it home?”

**15–20 · The verdict**

Tier 1: a small shrug. Tier 2: genuine appreciation. Tier 3: rare musical release.

T1 draft: “I’d leave that one.” T2 draft: “That’s a come-up.” T3 demo: “Wanz approved.” [Choose the song line together, with a clean alternate take.]

**20–25 · Return the question**

End on the object and one invitation.

“Would you wear it—or leave it? Show me what you found.”

**Store-run opening · alternate**

Enter with one constraint. Show the discovery before any sponsor information.

“One store. One thing worth bringing home. Let’s see what’s here.”

**Viewer-submission opening · alternate**

Credit the contributor and ask permission if adapting outside platform tools.

“You asked for a verdict. First, tell me the story.”

### Sample caption

The detail makes the difference. Would you wear it or leave it? Show me your find. #WanzApproved

## The Living Room Sessions

A weekly livestream where you take song requests, tell stories, work on songs, and let people know where they can see you perform.

A weekly show with four regular segments: requests, stories, songwriting, and upcoming appearances. Short excerpts become posts people can discover throughout the week.

**The format:** Weekly 60–90 minutes at a fixed slot on YouTube Live or Twitch. TikTok, Reels, and Shorts carry the excerpts back to the full experience.

**Who it’s for:** Listeners who know the hook, people who love hearing musicians work, and local audiences looking for a room to join.

**How it could earn:** Live appearances, tickets, and a newsletter people can join. Newsletter and show links give attention somewhere useful to go. Subscriptions are a later option, not the launch premise.

**What we would make:** One seated recording setup, a reliable vocal chain, chat moderation, four reusable segment titles, and a destination for show updates. Kevin Gardner’s participation is a proposed collaboration.

- Sing it in my voice — one request, interpreted with your musical instincts.
- What actually happened — one specific story from your own experience.
- Not Done Yet — hear a song develop with Kevin, if he joins.
- Seattle come see me — the next confirmed appearance and one clear invitation.

### Sample script

**00:00–00:20 · Cold open**

Camera begins on a live vocal phrase. Let the phrase resolve before a title appears.

[Sing a short selection chosen for this session.]

**00:20–01:00 · Welcome**

Medium shot. A small “The Living Room Sessions” title, then no clutter.

“Welcome to my living room. Tonight: a song you pick, a story I get to tell, and something I’m still working out. Put a request in the chat.”

**01:00–21:00 · Sing it in my voice**

Read a request, briefly name the musical approach, perform, then respond to chat.

“Let’s try that one a different way. Listen to what happens when we give it some room.”

**21:00–36:00 · What actually happened**

Hold the camera on you as you share a memory of your choice.

“Here’s a moment from that period I still think about.” [Tell a true story with a beginning, turn, and landing.]

**36:00–56:00 · Not Done Yet**

If Kevin joins, introduce the musical question before playing the work in progress.

“Kevin, let’s play the two versions. Tell us which one makes you want to hear the next line.”

**56:00–60:00 · Seattle come see me**

One verified appearance card. If there is no show to promote, use the newsletter invitation.

“If you’d like to hear this in a room together, here’s where I’ll be next. And tell me what you want in the next session.”

**Optional +30 minutes**

Extend requests by 15 minutes and songwriting by 15. The core 60-minute show still stands alone.

[Take another request; finish on music rather than housekeeping.]

### Sample caption

Song requests, stories, and new music. What would you bring to The Living Room Sessions?

## Not Done Yet, the Talk

A 30-minute talk about your working life and music, with three songs woven between the stories.

Tell the stories you want to tell about music, work, and continuing to make things. Choose three songs that belong with those stories. Offer it to organizations looking for a speaker who also performs.

**The format:** A 30-minute spoken keynote plus three songs. Plan approximately 42–45 minutes total, depending on song length; add a separate question period only if wanted.

**Who it’s for:** Corporate employee resource groups (ERGs), universities, Rotary, and audiences interested in creative life after 50.

**How it could earn:** A distinct speaking-and-music fee. A seated option may reduce stage demands, but preparation, travel, sound, and recovery time still count.

**What we would make:** An approved personal story arc, three songs that belong to it, a one-page talk description, a stage introduction, and a short teaser.

- The beginning that did not look like a beginning.
- What a different career taught you about making things.
- Not Done Yet — a question the audience can take home.

## Seattle Soul Circuit

A co-billed show with Bernadette Bascom or another Seattle musician: individual sets, shared stories, and a few songs together.

Start with one shared show. Each artist has a set, followed by music together. If the night works for the musicians and the room, it can become a recurring booking.

**The format:** Begin with one co-billed pilot, then consider a recurring residency. Bernadette Bascom is a proposed collaborator. Build toward a connected local circuit rather than isolated appearances.

**Who it’s for:** People already gathering around Seattle soul, regional music history, and community arts.

**How it could earn:** Co-billed performance fees or an agreed ticket split. Repeat nights can grow a dependable audience; the commercial terms come before promising a residency.

**What we would make:** One willing musical partner, one suitable room, a shared repertoire moment, a joint portrait or existing credited image, and a co-bill announcement template.

- A pilot evening with two distinct sets and one shared finale.
- A connection to Earshot, Folklife, or Bumbershoot programming where the format fits.
- City cultural proclamations as genuine community context, not a borrowed endorsement.

## Elder-to-Next-Gen

A filmed session where you and a younger artist work on a song with a DJ. Viewers hear the first take, the discussion, and what changes.

Work on one small part of a song together. Show the first version, one suggestion, and the next take. Leave room for everyone to teach and learn something.

**The format:** One recorded 30–45 minute workshop becomes a 45-second before-and-after and two short process clips. The DJ can also become the production partner for Remix Trend Riding.

**Who it’s for:** Younger musicians, listeners interested in the making of a song, and people who like watching musicians work together.

**How it could earn:** Initially a collaboration and audience-building format. Workshops, a joint performance, or a release are later possibilities if everyone wants them.

**What we would make:** A willing artist and DJ, one original phrase or demo, two repeatable takes, clearly agreed credits, and a shared understanding of what may be released.

- One line, two deliveries — a change in intention that you can hear.
- A voice against a new groove — preserve the performer’s identity.
- The reverse lesson — the younger artist or DJ shows you something you want to try.

### Sample script

**00–04 · Establish the challenge**

Split screen: singer / Wanz / DJ.

“Same line. Two different takes. Listen.”

**04–12 · Before**

Play the younger artist’s first take without ridicule or a fail graphic.

[Artist sings the original phrase.]

**12–22 · One suggestion**

Wanz offers one concrete, invited experiment.

“Who are you saying this to? Pick one person. Try it once just for them.”

**22–32 · Production response**

DJ adjusts one musical element, then leaves space.

DJ draft: “I’m going to pull this back so that line has somewhere to land.”

**32–40 · After**

Replay the same phrase at comparable volume.

[Artist performs the second take.]

**40–45 · Credit and invitation**

All collaborators named with equal care.

“What changed for you?” [Artist answers.] End text: “What should we try next?”

## Seattle Music Stories

Short interviews with Seattle musicians about the people, venues, and moments they remember. A longer conversation can also become a podcast.

Begin with a specific question about a place or person. Let the guest tell the story. Save the full conversation and make a few short excerpts that each tell a complete story.

**The format:** A 25–40 minute recorded conversation can produce three 30–60 second clips plus a full video or audio episode. Bernadette is a natural proposed first guest.

**Who it’s for:** Seattle-area listeners, musicians, local-history audiences, and people connected to the artists being named.

**How it could earn:** The first value is trust and a collection of recorded conversations. Live conversations, event bookings, and later series underwriting may follow; income would develop over time.

**What we would make:** Two good microphones, a quiet conversation, a meaningful photo or object, guest context, captions, and full credit. The supplied Bothell photo already gives the first visual opening.

- The room — a place that changed how someone performed.
- The person — who opened a door, challenged a habit, or taught a song.
- The version people missed — an artist’s own account, clearly identified as their memory.

## Remix Trend Riding

Take a current TikTok sound and add a bassline, a vocal phrase, or a soul influence from your own work. Let people hear the combination.

Work with a DJ to combine a current sound with a musical idea of your own. Play the source, introduce your part, and then play them together. The interest is in how the two fit.

**The format:** A 20–30 second native-platform experiment: original source cue, your new layer, then the combined idea. A remix or mashup, not a straight cover.

**Who it’s for:** People following the selected sound, production-minded listeners, and viewers drawn to a surprising musical connection.

**How it could earn:** A short video experiment that points back to your music or a collaborator. Judge it by attentive viewers and useful visits, not just borrowed trend visibility.

**What we would make:** A DJ or producer, one sound actually relevant at production time, a short new vocal or bass idea, and versions that let the original / added / combined layers be heard.

- A modern groove meets your bass-baritone.
- An operatic phrase changes the emotional scale.
- A 1970s soul vocabulary meets a current rhythmic idea, using material suitable for the intended use.

**00–03 · Set the collision**

**03–08 · Establish the source**

“Keep that there. Let me try something.”

[Combined musical idea.]

## Recording and editing workflow

Use transcription and editing tools to prepare clips and captions for review. You choose the final cuts and what gets posted.

A simple editing workflow turns one recording into several useful clips. Start small, review the results, and keep the steps that save time.

**The format:** Start with one recording: transcript → suggested moments → draft clips and captions → your review. Add scheduling and reporting once that process works.

**Who it’s for:** You and the people helping make and distribute the work. The behind-the-scenes process can also become an audience-facing episode.

**How it could earn:** Time returned to music and relationships. Measure how much repetitive work disappears before expanding the system.

**What we would make:** Your preferred tools, one source file, a naming convention, an folder of clips to review, and a simple list of where each approved version goes.

- Prepare: transcription, suggested moments, draft cuts, captions, and resizing.
- After review: scheduling, weekly results, and booking-email drafts.
- Your decisions: final cuts, collaborators, relationships, and fees.
